2 The four definitions of AI

The lecture opens with a definition pulling in two directions — understanding the nature of intelligence (science) and constructing intelligent systems (engineering) L1 00:00:46. Then two independent questions L1 00:01:53: is "intelligence" fidelity to humans or rationality? Is it about internal thought or external behavior? Two axes → four quadrants → four research fields. What the choice costs beyond lecture

Thinking rationally asks "is this inference valid?" — and fails silent under uncertainty (the qualification problem: "turning the key starts the car" has an endless list of exceptions; the frame problem: derive everything that did not change). Acting rationally asks "does this action maximize the objective?" — and now you must write down the objective. Section 10 shows you cannot, completely. Choosing this quadrant also commits the course to a real-valued performance measure — the utility function of §13 is implied here, in Lecture 1.

3 Acting humanly — the Turing Test

The setup — Alan Turing, 1950 L1 00:04:18

⌨

You, the interrogator

Typing at a terminal. 30 minutes, any questions you like — "practically any subject under the sun"

?→

A human

An AI system

If after 30 minutes you cannot reliably distinguish them, the AI is deemed intelligent. Breadth, not depth, is the demand — which is why it is "even today difficult to achieve" L1 00:04:56.

"Multiply these two 10-digit numbers" L1 00:05:33

human ~5 minutes, answer might be wrong
computer under 1 second, exactly right

"A wrong answer might mean that the respondent is human" L1 00:06:04 — so to pass, a machine must play slower and worse than it is.

Two structural consequences beyond lecture

  • • It is a matching criterion, not a maximizing one — it rewards resemblance, and resemblance requires imitating human weakness.
  • • It sets the ceiling at human level: a system twice as capable fails, if the excess is detectable.

Odd demands for an engineering discipline — part of why the field moved to a different quadrant.

To pass the modern version → six capabilities = the six subfields of AI L1 00:08:11

The test was constructed to require essentially everything — this list doubles as a table of contents for the field.

2 · Knowledge representation

Represent and store knowledge, symbolic or non-symbolic

3 · Automated reasoning

Draw conclusions by induction or deduction

4 · Machine learning

Detect and extrapolate patterns; adapt to new situations

5 · Vision + speech recognition

Perceive the world via cameras and microphones

6 · Robotics

Manipulate objects and move about in the world

4 Perfect → bounded rationality → satisficing

Perfect rationality is unattainable L1 00:18:14

"Always doing the right thing, all the time" — impossible in complicated environments: "you cannot possibly consider all the possible scenarios."

choose action
 └─ how long to think?
    └─ how long to think about that?
        └─ ⊥  infinite regress

beyond lecture For an agent embedded in time, "always do the right thing" is not demanding — it is ill-defined. Bounded rationality is the only coherent notion, not a sad compromise awaiting faster hardware.

Bounded rationality — Herbert Simon, 1957 L1 00:18:51

Originally an economic theory: consumers have limited rational decision making. Three drivers, each with a machine analogue:

Cognitive ability→ finite memory and processing
Time constraint→ a deadline on the decision
Imperfect information→ partial observability (§14)

Not three obstacles to remove one at a time — the three ways the world refuses to let you optimize. Nearly every technique in this course answers one of them.

Optimizing vs satisficing — same stream of options, two stopping rules L1 00:20:32

Candidates arrive one by one. The satisficer sets an aspiration level α and takes the first option that clears it. The optimizer must see them all before choosing the max.

✔

"Pwede na." L1 00:20:32

A suboptimal answer in seconds beats the optimal answer after a week — "we cannot wait for a week" L1 00:21:37. beyond lecture And once search cost is counted in the objective, satisficing can be the optimal policy — it is what optimizing looks like when the objective is honest about time.

The engineering answers — terminology L1 01:05:35

Anytime algorithm: interruptible at any time, value monotonically increases — answers Simon's time bound. Any-space algorithm: works in arbitrarily low memory, optimal on termination — answers the cognitive-capacity bound L1 01:06:14.

5 A brief history — expectation, collapse, recovery

Five phases L1 00:22:41. Learn the shape, not just the dates — the curve below is the field's enthusiasm over time. Click an era band for its milestones.

Which winter causes got fixed? beyond lecture

XOR was solved (multi-layer nets + backprop). Combinatorial explosion was never solved, only circumvented — we stopped searching exhaustively. Overconfidence has never been fixed, because it is sociological: impressive demo in a restricted setting → generalized claim → failure to scale → collapse. 7 The five laws of intelligent action

L1 00:53:11 beyond lecture: these are empirical generalizations from AI's first three decades (mostly Newell–Simon), not laws of physics — they summarize what has been observed to work.

LAW 1

Bounded rationality ⟹ opportunistic search

Under overload, agents use least-computation search, not optimal shortest-path search L1 00:53:41.

Makati traffic: main road = fewest km, stuck ✗ · side streets = more km + gas, arrive faster ✓
⟹ re-optimize the objective you can afford: time, not distance.

LAW 2 — contested

Physical symbol system: necessary and sufficient

Symbols realizable by engineered components; created, modified, reproduced, destroyed; interpretable as plans of action L1 00:55:27. "It really is debatable."

SUFFICIENT? unproven — hit scaling walls, not disproof
NECESSARY? in serious trouble — "no symbol there, they just [have] weights"

LAW 3

The magic number: 70,000 ± 20,000 chunks

An expert's knowledge base L1 00:56:33. Evidence: college vocabularies; expert systems grow to tens of thousands of rules; no world-class status without ~a decade of intense practice → the 10,000-hour rule; lifetime ≈ 2–3 domains.

beyond lecture The chunk is real (chess expertise); the 10,000-hour threshold is a popularization — deliberate practice is necessary, far from sufficient.

LAW 4

Search compensates for lack of knowledge

"Knowledge is the residue of search already performed" — a puzzle solved once needs no trial and error the second time L1 00:59:54. Deep Blue: tiny knowledge base (openings + endgames only), the rest "processed on the fly" — 200M positions/s × 120 s. And the crucial caveat: "not really the best move… the best is good enough move, given the effort" L1 01:01:37.

"take" → a shower | a book | a bus …
context disambiguates by exploring alternatives — that exploration IS search L1 01:02:12

LAW 5

Knowledge compensates for lack of search

"You avoid combinatorial explosion through knowledge" L1 01:03:22. Rubik's cube: practice → more knowledge → less search → less time. The Sphinx-3 ablation makes it quantitative:

Full system4% word error
Syntax off — a CONSTRAINT removed: the space GROWS30% (7.5×)
Frequency off — a RE-RANKER removed: same size, worse order6% (1.5×)

⟹ Knowledge that changes the size of the space dominates knowledge that changes only the order. Pruning beats re-ranking.

9 What an agent is — the loop

An agent perceives through sensors and acts through effectors — continually L2 00:01:53

"It's continually receiving percepts, processing these percepts, and generating actions that modify the environment" L2 00:03:06 — watch the dot disappear into the program and come out the other side as an action.

Human agent

Sensors: the five senses · Effectors: hands, legs, mouth · Environment: the physical world

Robot — "the prototypical intelligent agent"

Sensors: "the ubiquitous camera", laser range finder, microphone · Effectors: wheels, robot arm · Environment: the physical world

Softbot

10 Rational agents — and the measure you cannot write

A rational agent "acts rationally so as to achieve one's goals given one's beliefs" L2 00:06:31 — beliefs meaning knowledge about the environment, assumptions that make reasoning tractable and can be wrong: a ground robot models the world as a 2-D floor; a drone cannot L2 00:07:04.

Rationality is a FOUR-place relation — fix all four or the question is meaningless L2 00:11:21

1 · Performance measure

what counts as success

2 · Percept sequence

everything perceived so far — "experience"

3 · Knowledge of the environment

built-in by the programmer, or learned

4 · Actions available

"we don't expect a person to fly. But a bird has [that capability]"

— but NOT on anything not yet perceived L2 00:13:01

an ex ante standard: judged on information available at choice time, not on outcomes. The alternative would require omniscience. Asking "is this agent rational?" without fixing all four is like asking whether a number is large.

When you measure changes what you measure L2 00:09:47: a surprise exam measures study habits; an announced exam measures preparation + cramming. Same students, different construct.
Measured given what was perceived L2 00:10:48: ask the student questions unrelated to the lectures and they fail however hard they studied — you cannot be held to account for what you were never given.

The vacuum-cleaner failure — unintended consequences, live L2 00:14:36

Agent-side proxy: the agent sucks the dirt, spills it out again, collects it again… maximizing "dirt collected" perfectly while the floor stays exactly as dirty as it started.

The agent is not malfunctioning — the designer failed

Design measures over world states ("the floor is clean"), not agent-side quantities ("dirt collected") L2 00:14:03. beyond lecture — why this is structurally hard: (1) what we want is high-dimensional and mostly implicit (the qualification problem, on the objective side); (2) optimization pressure finds every gap between proxy and target — the optimizer searches the extremes, exactly where they diverge; (3) the proxy is easier to instrument — the path of least resistance runs toward the wrong choice. Crucial in reinforcement learning; a whole later course hangs on it L2 00:16:23.

Four notions, constantly confused L2 00:16:23

OMNISCIENCEknows the actual outcome of every action — impossible
PERFECTIONmaximizes actual performance — impossible outside toy worlds
RATIONALITY ★maximizes expected performance given percepts + knowledge — the achievable standard
AUTONOMYbehavior determined by own experience — a different axis entirely (§11)

Information gathering is part of rationality L2 00:17:28: acting to modify future percepts (a robot mapping a building). Zero immediate progress; every later decision better. Refusing to explore = optimizing a horizon shorter than your own lifetime. Returns as the problem generator in §13, and in force in Week 14 (RL).

11 Autonomy — the ability to adapt

Dictionary definition L2 00:20:14

"The ability to make your own decisions without being controlled by anyone else."

AI definition L2 00:20:45

"The ability to adapt to its environment — and this implies flexibility." Rely solely on built-in knowledge, disregard the percepts → the agent lacks autonomy.

The clock example — nobody controls a quartz clock, and it has zero autonomy L2 00:21:46

🕰

Quartz clock

Built-in mechanism only: crystal vibration ÷ circuits → display. Fly it across a time zone: "the clock will NOT adjust its time." Autonomous by the dictionary; not at all by the AI definition.

🛰

Smart clock with GPS

Senses and adapts — crosses the time zone and corrects itself. Its behavior is determined by what it perceives.

The operational definition is a DEGREE — "autonomous to the extent that its behavior is determined by its own experience" L2 00:24:18 — and "depends on percepts" is NOT the criterion beyond lecture:

NO PERCEPTS — the clock: behavior from built-in mechanism alone
PERCEPTS SELECT — the table-driven agent: percepts choose among responses fixed by the designer. After a million percepts, byte-identical.
PERCEPTS MODIFY — autonomous: experience changes the mapping itself. After a million percepts, a different agent.

Autonomy is orthogonal to rationality: a table-driven agent with a complete, correct table has perfect performance and zero capacity to adapt, simultaneously — only possible if they are separate axes. The rational agent is explicitly not omniscient: experience makes up for imperfect built-in knowledge L2 00:23:48.

Rung 0 — the table-driven agent L2 00:27:54 📊 slide 11/27

"Initially fully specified" is the whole objection in two words — the table must be complete before the agent starts. Four objections: ~10123 entries for chess; the designer must foresee every input; zero autonomy (an unforeseen change and it cannot act rationally); and learning does not rescue it — learning relocates the cost from design time to training time without shrinking the table. Verdict: only for highly constrained environments with no uncertainty.

📊 slide 11/27 — pseudocode (paraphrased aloud, shown verbatim on screen)
function TABLE-DRIVEN-AGENT(percept) returns an action
    persistent: percepts, a sequence, initially empty
                table, indexed by percept sequences, INITIALLY FULLY SPECIFIED
    append percept to the end of percepts
    action ← LOOKUP(percepts, table)
    return action

Rung 1 — the simple reflex agent L2 00:31:54 📊 slide 12/27

Acts on the current percept alone — "ignores the perceptual history." IF car in front is braking THEN initiate braking. The lecture's analogy: the makahiya plant — touch it, the leaves fold. Every time. No memory, no context.

Note INTERPRET-INPUT: the percept is first turned into a state — that seam is where the next rung is inserted.

EFFICIENCY"very efficient"
FLEXIBILITY"very limited"
📊 slide 12/27 — pseudocode, never read aloud
function SIMPLE-REFLEX-AGENT(percept) returns an action
    persistent: rules, a set of condition-action rules
    state  ← INTERPRET-INPUT(percept)
    rule   ← RULE-MATCH(state, rules)
    action ← rule.ACTION
    return action

Rung 2 — the model-based reflex agent L2 00:34:26 📊 slides 13–14/27

Internal state tracks the part of the world it cannot see now. The most useful sentence in Lecture 2 L2 00:35:33: it distinguishes world states that generate the same perceptual input but require different actions — a test you can apply, and a proof technique (Problem 4). Two models: transition = how the world moves; sensor = how the world looks to me. A self-driving car: the truck seen 2 s ago has advanced ~44 m (transition); empty LIDAR behind a guardrail means occlusion, not empty road (sensor).

EFFICIENCYstill rule-matching
FLEXIBILITYhandles partial observability
📊 slide 14/27 — read the arguments of UPDATE-STATE: the agent's own previous action is an input
function MODEL-BASED-REFLEX-AGENT(percept) returns an action
    persistent: state, transition_model, sensor_model, rules,
                action (the most recent action, INITIALLY NONE)
    state  ← UPDATE-STATE(state, action, percept, transition_model, sensor_model)

Rung 3 — the goal-based agent L2 00:38:25 📊 slide 15/27

The rules are DELETED and replaced by a projection box plus a goal — "What will it be like if I do action A?" Nothing is added to make it smarter; something is removed and computed instead. Reaching the goal needs long action sequences → planning, structured as search L2 00:39:38 — this is Week 3.

The hinge between the lectures L2 00:42:15: "search and knowledge are like opposites… and similarly here" — the reflex agent needs no search precisely because the designer supplied the knowledge. Laws 4+5, operating inside one agent's architecture. Consequences of the goal living in the designer's head: no rule for the unforeseen, and if the goal changes, every rule must be re-derived — a goal-based taxi accepts a destination it has never seen.

EFFICIENCY"not that efficient — it will have to do search"
FLEXIBILITY"more flexible"

Rung 4 — the utility-based agent L2 00:44:29 📊 slide 17/27

Not all goals are the same — Makati by taxi: arrival time vs safety vs fare, three conflicting objectives. A goal is a binary predicate; it is satisfied identically by a slow-cheap-safe trip and a fast-expensive-dangerous one. 📊 The slide adds the bridge the lecturer never states: "goals may be viewed as world states which make the agent happy" — so "how happy" is already meaningful, and utility U : State → ℝ is its answer: "the utility function provides the trade-off" L2 00:45:04. Decision rule 📊: "choose the action sequence that gives maximum utility."

beyond lecture Goals are a special case of utility (U = 1G) — this rung removes a restriction, it does not add a faculty. But a goal makes search a satisfaction problem (stop at first success); utility makes it optimization (no early stop). Expressiveness and cost are different axes.
Why real-valued? von Neumann–Morgenstern: preferences satisfying four modest axioms are representable as expected utility — violate them and you can be money-pumped. "Maximize expected utility" is not one rule among many. And it was implied back in Lecture 1, the moment rationality meant maximizing expected performance.

+ Learning — orthogonal to the ladder L2 00:45:37

"The learning agent is ANY type of agent… but this time it incorporates learning." It is not a sixth rung above utility-based — you can have a learning reflex agent or a learning utility-based agent. Each rung is defined by which objects it uses (rules g; state S,u,g; model T + goal G; T + utility U). Learning is a claim about where those objects come from — authored or acquired. Provenance is independent of type.

The problem generator exists because of information gathering (§10): an agent that only exploits never discovers a better option exists — "actions necessary for getting more information, so that in the future the agent performs better" L2 00:47:29.

The ladder, seen whole — each rung is a compression assumption on f : P* → A beyond lecture

The agent FUNCTION is a usually-infinite mathematical object (what the agent does). The agent PROGRAM is a finite artifact computing it (how). Every agent type answers: what structure does this environment have that lets me compress f?

Every real agent lives strictly between; Deep Blue makes the choice per region of one problem (openings/endgames stored, midgame searched).

14 Types of environment — six axes

L2 00:47:29 Each axis is a statement about what mathematics your problem requires. The green pole is the easy side; cross to red and a specific new machinery becomes mandatory beyond lecture.

Fully observable↔Partially

Sensors give the complete state at each point in time — equivalently, all aspects relevant to the choice of action, and relevance depends on the performance measure L2 00:48:37. Partial = unmeasured state or noisy, inaccurate sensors.

Crossing: state ≠ percept → you must maintain a belief over states. A cliff, not a step.

chess: fully · poker: partially — "you don't observe the hand of the other people"

Deterministic↔Stochastic

Next state completely determined by current state + the agent's action L2 00:52:02. Deterministic is easier: "less search, maybe no search at all"; stochastic → "a lot of search."

Crossing: a plan stops being a sequence and must become a policy π : S → A (see below).

chess: deterministic — all next moves determined L2 00:53:40

Episodic↔Sequential

Experience divided into episodes — "like a game over, then you start again"; action quality depends on the episode alone. Payoff: "planning is limited to one episode" L2 00:56:21. Caveat: that independence is for the performance measure, not learning — the X-ray analyzer is scored per patient and should still train on every previous one.

Crossing: you inherit the credit-assignment problem — which of a hundred earlier actions caused this outcome?

Static↔Semi↔Dynamic

Dynamic = changes while the agent deliberates. Semi-dynamic = the world holds still but the agent's performance doesn't: chess with a clock — "you could lose due to time default" L2 00:57:26. Consequence: keep sensing while deliberating — crossing the street while thinking about your exam.

Crossing: deliberation time enters the objective — anytime algorithms stop being optional.

Discrete↔Continuous

Discrete = a limited number of distinct, clearly defined percepts and actions L2 00:59:00. Chess squares vs taxi steering angle and speed.

Crossing: you cannot enumerate — function approximation becomes mandatory.

Single-agent↔Multi-agent

Others are treated as agents or just as objects (scenery) L2 00:50:21; as agents → cooperative or adversarial. Adversarial is strong: an agent that "tries its best so that you don't achieve your goal" — optimizing against your objective L2 00:51:27. That is what licenses minimax in Week 3; against a merely self-interested agent the assumption is wrong and needlessly pessimistic.

Crossing: optimization → game theory; the best action depends on what others do.

Sequence vs policy — what "plan" means beyond lecture

DETERMINISTIC → plan = SEQUENCE a₁, a₂, …, a_k
open loop — commit now, execute blind. Week 3's search assumes this.
STOCHASTIC → plan = POLICY π : S → A
closed loop — what to do in every state you might reach. The sequence is not even well-typed: "turn left, then accelerate" says nothing about the truck that is there after the turn.

16 Problems to work — click for the answer's shape

Work these with paper first. Each reveal gives the answer's shape; the full discussions are in notes-lectures-1-2-2026-08-20.md §Answers.

▸1 · Which clause of the definition rules out the table-driven agent — and why does a quartz clock fail it too?

"Novel situations." The table-driven agent's behavior is entirely the designer's anticipation written out in advance. The clock fails the same clause at the opposite extreme of competence — which tells you the clause is not about how good the system is, but whether competence extends past what was written in. §1 and autonomy (§11) are the same idea stated twice.

▸2 · PEAS for an insulin pump; break the simple reflex rule IF glucose > 180 THEN 2 U

Insulin stacking: readings 190→186→181 five minutes apart each fire the rule while earlier doses are still acting; by 11:30, 6 units act at once → glucose 35, seizure. Two world states ("2 U five minutes ago" vs "no active insulin") give the same percept but need different actions — the model-based criterion. Fix: track insulin-on-board (transition model) + treat the CGM as lagged and noisy (sensor model). Measure must be asymmetric: hypo kills in an hour, hyper is chronic.

▸3 · The syllogism is guaranteed correct — so why reject laws-of-thought? Which reason is strongest?

(i) inference is one way of acting rationally; (ii) we don't reason logically all the time; (iii) often no provably correct action exists, yet action is necessary. (iii) is strongest: the guarantee is conditional on correct premises and silent about where they come from — under uncertainty logic gives no answer, which for an agent that must act is worse than a wrong one. "Part of intelligence is knowing what to do when one does not know what to do."

▸4 · Prove no simple reflex agent can alternate turns on a beep

Two histories — "last turn LEFT" and "last turn RIGHT" — are observationally identical now (the percept is just beep) and behaviorally different now. g(beep) is one fixed action; contradiction. ∎ Smallest state space: one bit, S = {last LEFT, last RIGHT}. The gap between rung 1 and rung 2 is the gap between zero memory and any memory.

▸5 · Deep Blue vs the grandmaster on the storage↔computation spectrum

GM = knowledge end, paid at training time (a decade → ~70k chunks). Deep Blue = search end, paid at run time, again every move. The GM route is not simply better: acquisition is enormous and non-transferable (AlphaZero plays chess+Go+shogi; chunks don't), not copyable (a binary is), and the two routes fail differently — patterns fail silently on unfamiliar positions; search knows it was cut off. Complementary blind spots → modern systems sit in the middle.

▸6 · Which attribute makes vision > speech > chess — when chess has 10123 states?

State-space size is not one of the three attributes. Speech over puzzles: response time + data rate. Vision over speech: knowledge + data rate on the same clock. Chess's input is a handful of perfect, slow, discrete symbols — its difficulty is purely combinatorial, the kind we can attack (prune, order, cut off). Perception's input is continuous, noisy, ambiguous, incomplete — "chess is hard for a computer, perception is hard for a theory."

▸7 · The hospital model predicts future healthcare costs — who gets under-served?

Cost proxies care received, not illness. Patients who historically got less care for the same sickness (access, insurance, distance, unequal treatment) look cheap → low-risk → less extra care: the system reproduces the access gap it learned from. It defeats both standard responses: race was never an input, and the data are accurate. The defect is the choice of target variable — a human design decision. This is the vacuum cleaner at scale, with moral consequences.

▸8 · Why does Deep Blue store the opening and endgame but search the midgame?

A region is worth storing when it has high reuse + bounded size + expensive recomputation. Openings: every game starts identically (maximal reuse). Endgames: few pieces → exhaustively enumerable, many midgames funnel into them. The midgame fails reuse decisively — at ~10123, a position will likely never recur in the history of chess. The knowledge/search choice is made per region, not once per system.

▸9 · Build the reverse of poker: a stochastic world an agent may treat as deterministic

A robot arm with ±0.05 mm actuator noise and ±2 mm task tolerance: every outcome the agent can distinguish, and every outcome that matters to the measure, is the same outcome — the representation is coarser than the noise. Poker adds apparent randomness (sensors see less than the state); abstraction removes real randomness. Stops being legitimate the moment tolerance tightens past the noise — the classification changes without the world changing: these are properties of the agent–environment pair.

▸10 · How does a reflex agent achieve a goal it does not represent? What breaks if the goal changes?

The goal lives in the designer's head at design time, compiled into rules — IF braking THEN brake encodes "don't crash" without containing it. Forfeits flexibility; and since no rule refers to the goal, a goal change means re-deriving the entire rule set by hand. Reflex agent = compiled artifact; goal-based agent = interpreter — slower, reconfigurable at run time.

▸11 · Defend symbol-system necessity (attack the definition of "symbol")

"Symbol" was never restricted to discrete legible tokens — directions in a network's activation space designate, compose, and support systematically different behavior, so connectionism vindicates the hypothesis in an unexpected substrate. Unsatisfying because: it risks unfalsifiability (any working internals count), and the motivating properties were compositionality + systematicity, which distributed representations may have only approximately. The dispute migrated from "are there symbols?" to "does the system compose reliably?" — a better, testable question. "It really is debatable" is correct in 2026.

▸12 · Design a performance measure for an AI tutor for this course — then attack it

"Maximize exam score" → teach to the test (the vacuum cleaner — and the actual failure of the reviewer handout that preceded the notes). "Held-out transfer assessment" → ignores the student's time, motivation, retention horizon. Utility vector with explicit trade-offs → every term is still a proxy. The honest conclusion, by your own hand: you cannot write down what you want — so prefer world-state measures, unpredictable targets, cost terms, and a human who can notice the measure has come apart from the intent, because the agent by construction cannot.
